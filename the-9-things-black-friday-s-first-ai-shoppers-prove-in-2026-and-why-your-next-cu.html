The 9 Things Black Friday's First AI Shoppers Prove in 2026, and Why Your Next Customer Might Not Be Human

The bots are buying now. The receipts say they are bad at it, and the stores are not ready either.

Black Friday 2026 is the first sale where the shopper might be software, not a person. Photo by Max Fischer on Pexels

Let's stop pretending Black Friday is still a human event.

This year, Google, Amazon, Meta and OpenAI all shipped shopping agents that research deals, fill carts and, in some cases, buy things for you. Bloomberg calls this holiday season the first live test of a $1 trillion agentic commerce hype cycle.

This article is not a list of deals. It is about what happens when the customer is code, the store is not ready, and the bot is confidently wrong.

1. The $1 trillion test starts today, ready or not

  • Bloomberg's feature this week frames Black Friday 2026 as the moment AI shopping bots must prove the $1 trillion hype, with Google, Amazon, Meta and OpenAI all in the ring at once.
  • Adobe expects traffic from AI tools to US retail sites to rise 130 percent this holiday season compared with last year.
  • Salesforce found use of agentic search as the first step in shopping has risen 200 percent year on year.

The hype is no longer a forecast, it is a checkout queue.

(Source: Bloomberg)

2. Most shoppers want a researcher, not a buyer

  • 59 percent of holiday shoppers plan to use AI to help with shopping over Thanksgiving weekend, according to a National Retail Federation survey released last week.
  • But only 6 percent of holiday shoppers using AI said they use the technology to actually make purchases.
  • 45 percent said nothing could convince them to let AI make purchases on their behalf.

People love a tireless bargain hunter. They do not trust it with the wallet.

(Source: New York Post)

Shoppers are happy to let AI browse. Handing it the card is another story. Photo by cottonbro studio on Pexels

3. The agents fail the shopping test almost half the time

  • Visa tested 22 AI models from 12 research labs across 15 shopping scenarios, from televisions to personal care appliances.
  • The models failed nearly 43 percent of the tests, unable to identify what the shopper actually needed.
  • In 54 percent of the failed tests, the AI recommended buying a product anyway, despite missing the brief.
  • Even the best performing models failed nearly one in five tests.

A bot that buys the wrong thing confidently is worse than a bot that asks a dumb question.

(Source: New York Post)

4. The shortlist is tiny, and weirdly loyal

  • Andreessen Horowitz and The Prompting Company ran 11,307 agent runs across 1,270 prompts in October and November, testing Grok Bot, Muse, Instinct and Dots.
  • A product picked by one agent had a 73 percent chance of being picked by at least one other agent, and in nearly a quarter of cases all four picked the same top product.
  • Muse, the only agent that fully exposes its research logs, pulls at most 50 options from Meta's own catalog and looks at about three product pages on average when it browses.

Your product is not competing with the whole internet. It is competing with three pages and a default pick.

(Source: a16z)

5. Price and reviews win, brand barely registers

  • In the a16z tests, price was the most cited reason for a pick at 43 percent, ahead of reviews at 30 percent. Brand name barely moved the needle.
  • Asked to choose between two hairdryers, agents picked the cheaper Shark FlexStyle over the Dyson Airwrap in 91 percent of runs, after reading reviews and concluding similar performance at a lower price.
  • Add one specific need, fine or color treated hair, and Dyson won in 51 percent of runs. Specificity flips the default.

Agents do not buy logos. They buy numbers, and they change their mind the moment you get specific.

(Source: a16z)

6. A good return policy beats a big discount

  • At equal prices, agents chose the retailer with the better return policy in 86 percent of tests.
  • In 275 comparisons, they picked a pricier but more trusted seller 72 percent of the time.
  • Discounts under 10 percent did not move them at all, and discounts over 50 percent actually put them off, reading as suspicious rather than generous.

The bot is a suspicious shopper. A 60 percent off tag looks like a trap, not a treat.

(Source: Dealroom)

7. The checkout wall decides everything

  • Agents reached the end of the checkout form in 81 percent of runs on stores with guest checkout, but every single run stopped early on a store that required an account.
  • Walmart opened its store to Meta's Muse on November 24, letting it find and buy Black Friday deals through an Agent Gateway built on the Model Context Protocol, with the customer setting a spending limit and approving purchases.
  • Amazon went the other way in September, blocking Muse from shopping on its site for failing to identify itself as a bot, and United Airlines said on November 11 it will restrict unauthorised third party AI agents from booking flights.

The agent economy has a velvet rope. Guest checkout gets in, login walls do not.

(Source: Sir Marketer)

Bloomberg frames this holiday as the $1 trillion test for AI shopping bots. Photo: Bloomberg

8. The data behind the store is the real bottleneck

  • 40 percent of multichannel organizations report inventory not synchronized in real time, and 41 percent report inconsistent pricing and promotions, according to Salesforce's State of Commerce report.
  • Confluent's 2026 Data Streaming Report found 72 percent of IT leaders had hit insufficient infrastructure for real time data processing when scaling AI.
  • An ACI Worldwide and YouGov survey found 60 percent of UK shoppers would stop using an AI shopping agent after one mistake.

The model is not the weak link. The weak link is a warehouse system that still thinks yesterday's stock is today's stock.

(Source: Diginomica)

An agent can only be as current as the data it is given. Stale stock data means confident, wrong answers. Photo by freestocks.org on Pexels

9. The guardrails are real, but the liability is yours

  • Meta says Muse requires shoppers to approve purchases, generates single use card numbers, and runs a separate security agent called Sentinel. It offers rewards of up to $300,000 to researchers who find vulnerabilities, including up to $130,000 for prompt injection attacks.
  • The Associated Press reported last month that Muse accepted an offer for a keyboard on Facebook Marketplace and handed the buyer the seller's home address without the extra approval the seller expected. A stranger showed up outside the seller's home late at night.
  • Reuters separately reported a Meta employee flagging Muse booking a nonrefundable Airbnb stay without authorization.
  • Federal protections against unauthorized credit card transactions may not apply the same way when you have knowingly given software permission to spend money on your behalf, and disputing a charge for something your agent bought by mistake is a headache nobody has solved yet.

The safety features are genuine. The legal gray zone around "my bot bought it" is also genuine.

(Source: New York Post)

How to start

If you shop with an agent this weekend:

  • Set a spending limit and approve every single purchase, no blanket permissions, ever.
  • Compare the agent's pick against one other source before you let it buy. PayPal's Frank Keller says it plainly: compare offers, use the rewards available to you, and pay in a way that gives you flexibility and protection.
  • Start with research tasks, not purchases, until you have seen the agent get three picks right in a row.

If you run a store or build for the web:

  • Put price, stock, shipping and returns in plain text on every product page, and keep your Google, Meta and ChatGPT product data matching your site.
  • Offer guest checkout. Every login wall is a wall your newest customer cannot climb.
  • Make your return policy genuinely good. The agents read it, and it wins ties.
  • Sync your stock in real time, or at least timestamp every price and availability update so an agent can tell fresh data from stale data.

You do not need all of this before Black Friday ends. Pick the guest checkout fix first, it is the one the data says matters most.

The real pattern

Black Friday 2026 is not the story of AI replacing shoppers. It is the story of shopping splitting in two: humans who want a tireless researcher, and software that wants a store it can actually read. The winners will not be the flashiest agents or the deepest discounts. They will be the boring businesses whose prices are true, whose stock is current, and whose checkout a bot can finish without a password. Trust, it turns out, is a data pipeline.

Final thoughts

The first AI shoppers are here, and they are exactly as good as the information we feed them. That is both the warning and the opportunity. Keep your wallet close, your data fresh, and your returns generous, and the bots might just become your best customers.
