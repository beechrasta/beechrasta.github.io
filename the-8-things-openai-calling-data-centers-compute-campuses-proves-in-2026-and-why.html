The 8 Things OpenAI Calling Data Centers Compute Campuses Proves in 2026, and Why a New Name Changes Nothing

Your chatbot runs in a building your neighbours hate. Now the company behind it wants you to call that building something prettier.

A protest sign against AI data centers in Vancouver, Canada, on June 27, 2026. Credit: Ethan Cairns, Bloomberg via Getty Images.

Let's stop pretending this is a branding accident.

OpenAI does not want you to say data center anymore. Its new preferred term is compute campus, confirmed by a company spokesperson to Business Insider on November 1. The power draw stays the same. The water use stays the same. Only the label gets a haircut.

This article is not a dictionary update. It is about why the most powerful AI company on Earth suddenly cares what you call its buildings, and what that tells you about the fight coming in 2028.

1. What OpenAI actually said, and when it said it

  • TechCrunch reported on November 1 that OpenAI is preparing a post-midterm public relations push, citing Axios.
  • Chief global affairs officer Chris Lehane discussed rebranding data centers as AI compute campuses as one proposal among several.
  • A spokesperson then confirmed the new term to Business Insider, saying compute campuses better describe the scale and role of the infrastructure being built for AI.

The key phrase is preferred term, not new product. Nothing was announced about a single building changing how it operates.

(Source: TechCrunch, OpenAI might rebrand its data centers as AI compute campuses)

2. The numbers that forced the rebrand are brutal

  • Pew Research Center surveyed 10,548 US adults from July 20 to August 9, 2026.
  • 54 percent now say data centers are mostly bad for the environment, up from 39 percent in January.
  • 50 percent say they are mostly bad for home energy costs, up from 38 percent, and 49 percent say they are mostly bad for quality of life nearby, up from 30 percent.
  • Only 4 percent see a mostly good impact in each of those three areas, and 60 percent say they would be uncomfortable with a new data center near them.

You do not rebrand a popular product. You rebrand the thing voters have started organising against.

(Source: Pew Research Center via Help Net Security, Americans views of data centers turn more negative)

Server racks inside a data center. Credit: Adobe Stock via NPR.

3. This is not even the first rename of the year

  • In September 2026, President Trump decided he prefers Super Intelligence to artificial intelligence, and pushed its use among federal officers.
  • Gizmodo noted that OpenAI CEO Sam Altman is notably absent from the leaders who adopted that term.
  • Now OpenAI has its own preferred vocabulary, for buildings instead of brains.

When everyone in power starts renaming things at once, the renaming is the strategy.

A new word is cheaper than a new power grid, and it ships faster.

(Source: Gizmodo, OpenAI would reportedly like you to use its pleasant new term for data centers)

4. The midterms turned a building permit into a campaign issue

  • The US midterm elections fall on Tuesday, November 3, 2026, and Axios reports OpenAI is planning its push for after them.
  • Lehane told Axios the midterms will be a bit of a movie trailer for what you will see in 2028, when he expects AI to be the issue in the presidential election.
  • Gizmodo points out the political logic plainly: calling someone a data senator is a great insult, while calling someone a compute campus senator has no ring to it at all.

The audience for this rebrand is not engineers. It is voters, local councils, and the candidates who will need their votes in 2028.

(Source: TechCrunch, OpenAI might rebrand its data centers as AI compute campuses)

5. Rivals already tried the softer playbook, and it was not a rename

  • Amazon and Microsoft have both said they will no longer use nondisclosure agreements when negotiating data centers with local governments.
  • Amazon and Meta have each announced about 1 billion dollars in community incentives, and Google pledged local program investments reaching 1 billion dollars by 2027.
  • TechCrunch reported in October that Amazon buried its NDA change inside a longer blog post arguing data centers are good for communities.

OpenAI watched its rivals buy goodwill with transparency and cash, then chose a cheaper currency: language.

(Source: TechCrunch, Can the AI industry persuade data center opponents by getting rid of NDAs?)

6. Campus was already the industry's favourite euphemism

  • In October 2026, OpenAI and Oracle unveiled a 7 billion dollar data center in Michigan, celebrated as the state's first hyperscale campus.
  • Officials in Wythe County, Virginia, announced a data center campus, and AVAIO Digital declared plans for an AI-ready data center and power campus in Little Rock, Arkansas.
  • The new term therefore does not invent a category. It standardises the nicest word the industry already uses when it wants planning permission.

Campus sounds like students and trees. A data center sounds like humming boxes and a power bill. That gap is the whole point.

(Source: Career Ahead AI, OpenAI discusses AI compute campuses)

Inside a large data center. Credit: IBM.

7. A rename changes nothing physical, and that is the uncomfortable truth

  • Power draw, water use, and noise do not change when you change the sign on the fence.
  • A Senate Democratic report released on October 9, 2026, criticised major data center operators for not covering the grid costs their facilities trigger, and for seeking tax breaks.
  • Roughly 300 local governments had already paused data center approvals while that debate played out.

Call it a campus, a facility, or a palace. The meter still spins, and someone still pays for the grid upgrade.

Residents are not angry at a word. They are angry at bills, heat, noise, and decisions made behind closed doors.

(Source: Senate report summary, Who pays for the AI data center boom?)

8. What would actually change minds is boring, and therefore avoided

  • Lehane himself said communities should hold companies responsible and accountable so they really and truly get a transformative benefit from these projects.
  • That sentence is the test. A transformative benefit is published power and water numbers, no secret negotiations, and community money in writing before construction, not after the protest.
  • Amazon and Microsoft dropping NDAs at least changes what residents can see. A new name changes only what residents are asked to say.

If OpenAI wants the word campus to stick, it has to build something that behaves like a neighbour, not just a brand.

(Source: TechCrunch, OpenAI might rebrand its data centers as AI compute campuses)

How to start

  • If you live near a proposed site, ask for three numbers in writing: power draw, water use, and who pays for grid upgrades.
  • If you build AI products, stop repeating vendor euphemisms in your own docs. Your users can tell when a data center is being hidden from them.
  • If you vote in the US, watch what candidates say about data centers after November 3. The 2028 version of this argument is being rehearsed right now.
  • First step this week: read the Pew numbers above before your next argument about AI infrastructure. They are the reason this rebrand exists.

The real pattern

Every industry that loses the public argument renames the thing first and fixes the thing never, and AI just reached that stage faster than most because its buildings arrived in neighbourhoods before its benefits arrived in pay packets.

Final thoughts

A compute campus is still a data center with better PR. Judge it by its meter readings and its contracts, not its vocabulary. The next time a company offers you a prettier word, ask what it is trying not to say.
