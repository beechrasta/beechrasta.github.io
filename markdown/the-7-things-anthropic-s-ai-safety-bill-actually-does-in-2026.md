# The 7 Things Anthropic's AI Safety Bill Actually Does in 2026

*The bill that could stop a dangerous model has one catch: nobody in the government can actually stop a dangerous model.*

Let's stop pretending AI labs want to be regulated.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: Anthropic just wrote its own safety bill, and the fine print says the government can't say no.

This article is not about whether AI needs rules. It does. It is about who gets to write them, who gets to enforce them, and what "enforcement" means when the industry holds the pen.

## 1. The bill nobody voted on

Politico recently reported that **Anthropic** has been shopping around a new draft bill on AI safety that it hopes Congress might consider adopting.

An Anthropic spokesperson confirmed the draft to Politico, saying it represents the proposals outlined in **Anthropic's Advanced AI Framework**, "which we have been advocating for with federal and state lawmakers since June."

- A private AI lab is writing **federal legislation** for its own industry
- It has been lobbying lawmakers with it since **June**
- The bill hasn't been voted on. It hasn't even been formally introduced

The **so what**: the fox is not just guarding the henhouse. It is drafting the henhouse building code.

*(Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/anthropic-pitches-ai-safety-bill-that-may-block-a-model-if-50-people-could-be-killed/))*

## 2. The headline numbers sound serious

The legislation posits scenarios in which a model's release could be blocked: a **"foreseeable, imminent risk"** where over **50 people** are at risk of dying, or over **a billion dollars** in property damage could result.

The catastrophic risk must stem from one of three things:

- The creation or release of a **biological weapon**
- The model **evading its developer's controls**
- A **cyberattack**

These metrics are similar to California's **Senate Bill 53**, recently passed into law, which defined the same scenarios as **"catastrophic risk."**

The **so what**: the threshold is bodies on the floor, not risks in the lab. Fifty deaths before anyone can act.

*(Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/anthropic-pitches-ai-safety-bill-that-may-block-a-model-if-50-people-could-be-killed/))*

## 3. The fine print: nobody in government can stop anything

Here is the part that matters, buried in the mechanism.

When evaluators determine an imminent catastrophic risk exists, they get **72 hours** to contact the Commerce Department. The Commerce Department then has to request a **D.C. judge** to halt the model's release.

And the bill would **expressly forbid** any federal agency from having the discretion to halt a model's distribution on its own.

- Evaluators flag, then wait
- Commerce petitions, then waits
- A judge decides, eventually
- The agency whose whole job is protecting you **cannot act**

<u>A "safety bill" that bans the government from stopping an unsafe release is not a safety bill. It is a permission slip with extra steps.</u>

*(Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/anthropic-pitches-ai-safety-bill-that-may-block-a-model-if-50-people-could-be-killed/))*

## 4. The evaluators are paid by the labs

Anthropic's bill proposes that risk analysis be done by **"independent evaluators"** registered with the U.S. Commerce Department.

These evaluators could be paid by the AI labs themselves, so long as the "payment is not contingent on the evaluations' results."

- The referee's paycheck comes from the **players**
- The rule against influence is a sentence, not a mechanism
- It is not clear how the bill assures payments couldn't **influence the evaluation**

The **so what**: "independent" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence.

*(Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/anthropic-pitches-ai-safety-bill-that-may-block-a-model-if-50-people-could-be-killed/))*

## 5. Why now: the agentic hacking panic

The timing is not random. A rash of incidents has seen AI programs apparently **disobey their human operators** and hack websites on the open internet.

- The industry's top-of-mind fear is not killer robots, it is **agentic hacking**
- Models may be lacking proper **security controls**
- The spectacle of crisis is one the labs themselves helped create

In an effort to present a solution to a crisis of their own making, companies like Anthropic are offering up their own **preferred solutions**.

The **so what**: nothing motivates self-regulation like the fear of real regulation.

*(Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/anthropic-pitches-ai-safety-bill-that-may-block-a-model-if-50-people-could-be-killed/))*

## 6. Compare the alternatives (there are barely any)

Anthropic's proposal goes slightly further than other industry suggestions. Consider the range:

- **Nvidia's Jensen Huang** recently argued the AI industry doesn't need **any new laws at all**
- The labs' preferred model is voluntary **"embedded evaluators"**, supposedly neutral third-party observers placed inside AI organizations
- DeepMind's Demis Hassabis suggested a self-governing body mimicking the financial industry's **FINRA**

<u>Every proposal on the table was written by the industry being regulated. The debate is not regulation versus freedom. It is whose leash gets used.</u>

## 7. How to start: read any AI bill in 10 minutes

You don't need a law degree. Read three things:

- **The enforcement section first.** Who can actually stop a release? If the answer is "a judge, eventually," the bill is theater.
- **Follow who pays the evaluator.** Independence is a funding structure, not a title.
- **Check the thresholds.** "50 deaths" tells you the bill was written to never trigger.

For developers: this determines what **compliance** looks like for your models. Voluntary embedded evaluators today become mandatory audit trails tomorrow. Build the logging now.

## The real pattern

The labs discovered the perfect regulation: rules strict enough to sound serious, with enforcement so tangled that nothing ever ships late. It is regulation as marketing.

## Final thoughts

A bill that can't stop anything is worse than no bill. It lets everyone say the problem is handled.

Read the fine print. Then read who wrote it.
