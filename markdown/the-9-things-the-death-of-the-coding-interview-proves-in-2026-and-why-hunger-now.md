# The 9 Things the Death of the Coding Interview Proves in 2026, and Why Hunger Now Beats Tenure

Forget LeetCode marathons. The most honest hiring signal in tech right now is a weekend prototype built by someone nobody in the room listens to yet.

![Claude Code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/beechrasta/medium-images/main/2026-11-01/claude-code-hero.webp)
*Claude Code, the AI coding tool at the center of Anthropic's hiring rethink. Credit: Despertar Matinal.*

Let's stop pretending the coding interview measures what it used to measure.

An **Anthropic** engineering manager just killed it for his team, for a reason nobody can argue with: **Claude** is now better at the test than the candidates taking it. And the replacement he proposes is both thrilling and slightly terrifying for every junior engineer reading this.

This article is not a eulogy for whiteboard interviews. It is about what replaces them, who wins, and who gets quietly locked out.

## 1. The test died the day the product aced it

**Felix Rieseberg**, an engineering manager at **Anthropic** who has led engineering for **Claude.ai**, **Cowork**, and **Claude Code Desktop**, wrote on October 27 that he has stopped asking candidates to do traditional coding interviews.

His line: "This month, I laid the traditional coding interview to rest."

He is not being dramatic for clicks. Small coding challenges were once a weak but useful predictor of success on his teams. That signal is gone, because the thing being tested is now done better by the company's own product.

*(Source: [Felix Rieseberg, Hunger beats tenure](https://felixrieseberg.com/hunger-beats-tenure-advice-for-junior-engineers/))*

## 2. A React challenge is now as useful as an assembly quiz

Rieseberg's killer comparison: "There's no more point in giving you a little React challenge than there is in quizzing people about assembly; Claude is now better at both."

Read that twice. He is not saying coding is dead. He is saying **the test measures a skill that is no longer scarce**.

His most impactful builders, he writes, succeed regardless of how good they are at these tests. If your filter cannot separate your best people from your average ones, it is not a filter. It is a ritual.

*(Source: [Felix Rieseberg, Hunger beats tenure](https://felixrieseberg.com/hunger-beats-tenure-advice-for-junior-engineers/))*

## 3. The replacement is a prototype, not a puzzle

So what does Rieseberg look at instead? Small prototypes in resumes and portfolios that show **taste, empathy, and principled thinking**.

That is a much harder thing to fake than a sorting algorithm, and a much more revealing thing to build. A prototype shows what problem you chose, what you left out, and who you were thinking about when you built it.

A timed puzzle shows you can perform under artificial pressure. A prototype shows you can **decide what matters**.

*(Source: [The Register, Anthropic engineer declares the coding interview dead](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/28/anthropic-engineer-declares-the-coding-interview-dead/5306115))*

## 4. Two newcomers proved it over a single weekend

Rieseberg's star example is from his own company. Two relatively new **Anthropic** engineers, **Anthony Morris** and **Alice Zhao**, built a working prototype of a graphical interface for **Claude Code** over one weekend.

A public version shipped about eight days later.

No requirements document. No lobbying for designer time. No six week approval chain. Two people without status saw something missing, built it, and shipped it. That is the whole thesis in one anecdote: **execution is no longer the expensive part**.

*(Source: [The Decoder, entrepreneurial thinking at Anthropic](https://the-decoder.com/with-ai-writing-the-code-entrepreneurial-thinking-is-now-what-counts-most-for-newcomers-at-anthropic/))*

## 5. Newcomers see what veterans stopped noticing

This is the oldest idea in the post, and the most underrated. Rieseberg leans on organizational researcher **James March**, who wrote in 1991 that organizations depend on a steady stream of naive newcomers.

Veterans stop noticing friction. They learned to live with it years ago. Newcomers trip over it on day one.

At **Notion**, Rieseberg was once asked to design an offline architecture everyone inside considered impossible, partly because nobody had ever actually asked the founder whether the supposedly immovable objection was real. It was not. The technical hurdle was real, but the resistance was folklore.

<u>Fresh eyes are not a soft skill. They are an information advantage.</u>

*(Source: [The Decoder, entrepreneurial thinking at Anthropic](https://the-decoder.com/with-ai-writing-the-code-entrepreneurial-thinking-is-now-what-counts-most-for-newcomers-at-anthropic/))*

## 6. His five step playbook for engineers without power

Rieseberg is explicit that "junior" is the wrong word. His advice is for **anyone without social capital, status, or power** inside a company. The playbook:

- **Pick business impact over backlog.** Learn your manager's manager's goals, and attach yourself to the parts of the company that make money.
- **Pick a radical idea.** Something that would dramatically improve the odds of hitting those goals, not a safe incremental ticket.
- **Build it over a weekend.** Skip the requirements doc, the buy in meetings, and the wait for a designer. A working prototype beats a polished proposal.
- **Keep it to one idea.** Juniors, he says, often hide three excellent ideas among 20 bad ones. One sharp idea wins.
- **Use the outsider's view.** You understand outside users better than veterans do, and you have not yet learned all the reasons something is supposedly impossible.

The catch, which he states bluntly: "The only problem with all that ignorant power: Nobody cares what you think." The prototype is how you make them care.

*(Source: [Felix Rieseberg, Hunger beats tenure](https://felixrieseberg.com/hunger-beats-tenure-advice-for-junior-engineers/))*

## 7. The uncomfortable number hiding behind the optimism

Here is the part the celebratory headlines skip. Eleven days before Rieseberg's post, **Anthropic** published research on its own hiring: junior candidates fell from **27 percent** of technical hires in early 2025 to **9 percent**, even as the company kept growing.

That does not prove AI caused the drop. But it means the "hunger beats tenure" era is arriving at the exact moment junior doors are narrowing. Hunger only beats tenure if newcomers get hired in the first place.

And this is one manager's decision for his own team, not company policy. **Anthropic's** careers guidance still says technical candidates may be assessed with live coding tools like **Colab** and **CodeSignal**.

*(Source: [FirstPost, Anthropic manager scraps coding interviews](https://www.firstpost.com/tech/anthropic-manager-scraps-coding-interviews-says-claude-makes-it-pointless-14055920.html))*

## 8. The rest of the industry is moving, just more cautiously

Rieseberg is not alone. **Google** principal engineer **Jaana Dogan** responded that she would rather interview candidates on how they use coding agents, because that itself reveals more about their coding skills.

But the industry at large is not abandoning tests. **ZipRecruiter's** October 2026 employer survey of 1,500 employers found **62.5 percent** of Information sector employers still require formal skill tests on initial applications, and **70 percent** have switched to proctored or in person assessments to deter AI cheating.

The same survey found **61.5 percent** of Information employers say critical thinking is the primary skill missing from today's candidate pool. That is the real hiring crisis: not that candidates cannot code, but that so few can think.

*(Source: [The Register, Anthropic engineer declares the coding interview dead](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/28/anthropic-engineer-declares-the-coding-interview-dead/5306115))*

## 9. The catch nobody wants to say out loud

"Hunger beats tenure" sounds like pure empowerment. It also quietly moves the bar from diligence and craft to entrepreneurial thinking, product sense, and taste.

Universities barely teach those skills. Companies traditionally expected them from experienced product managers or founders, not from 22 year olds. And the model examples, prototypes built on weekends without being asked, assume a hustle capacity that not every new developer can bring to the job.

There is also a legal shadow. Tech companies have gotten into trouble before for pushing out expensive veterans in favor of cheaper, hungrier young workers. "Hunger beats tenure" is a great blog title and a dangerous HR slogan if a company reads it literally.

<u>The skill shift is real. The hype around it needs a seatbelt.</u>

*(Source: [The Register, Anthropic engineer declares the coding interview dead](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/10/28/anthropic-engineer-declares-the-coding-interview-dead/5306115))*

## How to start

- **If you are a junior engineer:** stop grinding puzzle 400. Ship one small prototype that solves a real problem for real users, and put it where hiring managers can see it.
- **If you are a student:** your final year project is your interview now. Pick one sharp idea, build it end to end with AI tools, and write about what you chose and why.
- **If you are a hiring manager:** try one hiring loop with no coding test. Ask for a prototype and a walkthrough of the decisions behind it, and compare the signal honestly.
- **If you are a senior engineer:** mentor one newcomer this month. Their outsider question about your system might be the most valuable review you get all year.

## The real pattern

The coding interview did not die because engineers got worse. It died because the scarce skill changed. Writing code was the bottleneck, so we tested writing code. Now choosing the right problem, understanding the user, and shipping a decision are the bottleneck, and the old test cannot see any of them. Every hiring ritual built for the old bottleneck will fall the same way, one manager at a time.

## Final thoughts

I have watched friends spend months memorizing algorithms for interviews that a chatbot now aces in seconds. That time was not wasted, exactly, but it was aimed at the wrong target.

Build something small this weekend. Something with one idea, for one kind of user, that actually works. Then show it to someone who can say yes. That is the new interview, and unlike the old one, you can take it as many times as you want.
