The 9 Things AI Eating Junior Jobs Proves in 2026, and Why the Career Ladder Lost Its First Rung

The bosses are not whispering about it anymore. More than half of them now say AI is cutting headcount, and the first rung of the ladder is where the axe falls.

The entry level job was how juniors learned the trade. In 2026, the trade is being automated first. Photo by Thirdman on Pexels

Let's stop pretending the AI jobs debate is about the distant future.

This week, a global study found more than half of business leaders say AI is already reducing headcount, and the junior roles are shrinking fastest. Banks are hiring coding agents instead of developers. The numbers come from payroll records, not predictions.

This article is not a list of layoff headlines. It is about who exactly is losing the first rung, why the bosses admit it now, and what still gets a junior hired.

1. The bosses just said the quiet part out loud

  • BSI's new global research, polling 1,287 business leaders across nine countries, found 54 percent now say AI is helping to reduce headcount, up from 41 percent in 2025.
  • More than half say AI is helping to reduce junior headcount specifically, up from 45 percent last year.
  • 49 percent say AI has already reduced entry level opportunities, and 53 percent say AI is already being used to justify efficiencies, streamlining, job cuts or recruitment freezes.

This is not a forecast anymore. It is a confession, with a sample size.

(Source: BSI)

2. The junior rung is shrinking fastest

  • Mercer's Global Talent Trends report, surveying about 12,000 executives, HR leaders and employees, found more than 99 percent of executives expect AI to lead to at least some layoffs within the next two years.
  • The proportion of companies reducing junior positions rose from 17 percent to 43 percent within a single year.
  • Young professionals aged 22 to 27 are considered particularly exposed, because AI now does the routine analytical and administrative work that used to train new hires.

The layoffs are not evenly spread. They are aimed at the bottom of the org chart.

(Source: People Matters)

The first job was never about the output. It was about learning how the work works. Photo by Alena Darmel on Pexels

3. This is payroll data, not vibes

  • A five year study from Stanford's Digital Economy Lab and King's College London, covering 1.25 billion job postings and 154 million worker records across 41 countries from 2021 to 2026, found AI adopting firms raised senior employment by 6.7 percent while cutting junior positions by 2.5 to 3 percent.
  • Total headcount at those same firms actually grew 3.3 percent. Junior workers saw their share of total employment fall by 1.9 percentage points.
  • The researchers' own summary is blunt: AI acts as a lever for experience and a replacement for apprenticeship.

Companies are not shrinking. They are getting top heavy, and the entry door is quietly closing.

(Source: NRB)

4. Banks stopped hiring coders and started supervising agents

  • Wells Fargo has said it is already choosing autonomous coding agents over hiring additional employees or engaging contractors, according to a Kotak Institutional Equities report covered by ET BFSI this week.
  • Most financial services firms report productivity improvements of 20 to 40 percent in software development from AI adoption. PNC indicated agentic AI could deliver improvements of five to ten times.
  • Visa said its stablecoin platform was designed, built and shipped in seven weeks with 12 people, using its agentic toolchain, and 60 percent of its global product development and engineering teams have adopted agentic AI tools.
  • Citi reported 14,000 engineers using its AI coding tool Devin, cutting technology vendor spending 30 percent year on year and launching products 30 to 40 percent faster.

The bank does not need ten juniors to write the code anymore. It needs two seniors to supervise the agents that write it.

(Source: ET BFSI)

5. The Indian IT angle nobody wants to say out loud

  • The same Kotak report warns that rising productivity from agentic AI puts Indian IT services firms at risk of further deflation in their base business, even as aggregate BFSI revenues grew 6.1 percent year on year in the September 2026 quarter.
  • At Zeta, co-founder Ramki Gaddipati told The Economic Times that nobody on his engineering team opens an IDE to write code anymore. Agents generate the code, agents check the pull request first for security and run the full regression suite, and the human reviews only after the agents clear it.
  • Zeta reports a straightforward 30 to 40 percent productivity boost, and says the number of people needed to deliver services to clients has fallen by over 50 percent.

India's biggest fresher employer pipeline, IT services, is exactly the work agents are eating first.

(Source: Economic Times)

6. The ladder was a classroom, and the classroom is closing

  • 49 percent of leaders in the BSI study worry career starters are not developing the right skills because they consult AI rather than a manager.
  • 42 percent would be less likely to promote someone who sends them AI generated content, while 49 percent think colleagues are putting in less effort because they can rely on AI.
  • Researchers at the London School of Economics, studying 243 million new hire records and 407 million job adverts, point out why: the tasks generative AI mirrors most closely, summarising text, researching, keeping meeting minutes, are exactly the tasks that used to fall to a firm's newest recruits.

You cannot learn judgement from a task you never did. That is the part the efficiency math never prices in.

(Source: LSE Business Review)

Interviews still happen. But the questions are changing, from "can you do this task" to "can you judge this output". Photo by Tima Miroshnichenko on Pexels

7. The counterpoint the doomers skip

  • The Stanford study's most important number is the one nobody quotes: total headcount at AI adopting firms grew 3.3 percent. AI is concentrating work at the top, not eliminating work altogether.
  • The LSE researchers warn of a confound: the jobs most exposed to AI are also the jobs that went remote fastest, so some of the junior hiring decline may be work from home, not the model.
  • Gartner's research, cited in the Mercer coverage, found similar workforce reduction rates at firms with strong AI returns and weak ones. Cutting heads does not automatically buy better financial results.
  • A fresh German study from ZEW Mannheim and the Oxford Internet Institute, surveying managers at 1,435 firms, found willingness to use AI instead of a junior hire falls sharply when the tasks require judgement rather than routine. 31.3 percent expect fewer entry level hires because of AI, not all of them, and not for every task.

The honest version is narrower than the panic: routine junior work is being automated, judgement work is not, and the layoffs do not even guarantee the savings.

(Source: People Matters)

8. The two tier workplace is already here

  • 28 percent of organizations in the BSI study already align pay and promotion with employee adoption of AI, and 41 percent of leaders felt pressure from senior leadership to adopt it.
  • 81 percent of leaders say they use AI to be more efficient in their role, yet 51 percent admit they often use it without considering whether it is the best option, and 57 percent say AI has become their default choice where previously they would have engaged a colleague.
  • 64 percent suggest AI can help low skilled employees hide performance or competence issues, up from 53 percent a year ago, while 75 percent of firms have experienced at least one AI failure and 42 percent of leaders use AI tools not officially approved by their organization.

The new office politics is simple: the people who can wield AI get promoted, the people who lean on it get suspected, and the line between the two is invisible.

(Source: BSI)

The degree still matters. The proof of what you can do with AI matters more. Photo by Pavel Danilyuk on Pexels

9. What still gets a junior hired

  • Judgement over routine. The ZEW study is clear: when a task needs judgement, managers stop reaching for the model. Build the judgement muscle, reviewing outputs, catching errors, deciding what good looks like, and you are competing where AI is weakest.
  • Proof over credentials. As IT Brief Asia put it this week, AI broke the job application, and proof is what replaces it. LinkedIn was receiving around 11,000 applications a minute in 2025, up 45 percent on the year before, with AI tools a large part of the reason. A shipped project, a public repo, a real user, these now beat a polished CV, because a CV is exactly the kind of text AI writes best. Microsoft and LinkedIn's 2024 Work Trend Index found 66 percent of business leaders would not hire someone without AI skills, and 71 percent would rather hire a less experienced candidate with AI skills than a more experienced one without them.
  • Agent fluency as a skill, not a shortcut. Zeta now measures engineers against explicit agent fluency standards. The juniors who win will be the ones who can direct agents, verify their work, and own the result, not the ones who paste output and hope.
  • Pick the rooms where juniors still get hired. Banks are automating, but someone still has to supervise, validate and take responsibility. Aim for roles where a human signs off, because the signature is the job now.

The rung is not gone. It moved, from doing the task to judging the task, and the sooner you climb to the new rung, the better.

(Source: IT Brief Asia)

How to start

If you are a student or a junior, a 90 day plan:

  • Days 1 to 30: pick one real problem and ship something small with an AI coding agent, then write up what the agent got wrong and how you caught it. That write up is your proof.
  • Days 31 to 60: learn to supervise, not just prompt. Review agent pull requests, run the tests, break the output on purpose and fix it. Zeta's agent fluency idea is a good checklist to steal.
  • Days 61 to 90: put the proof where hiring managers look, a public repo, a short demo video, a blog post. One strong artifact beats ten applications.

If you manage juniors:

  • Protect the pathways to expertise. BSI's own report lists it as a core action: design AI enabled work so learning, judgement and progression survive, especially for entry level staff.
  • Give juniors the judgement tasks, not just the leftover routine. If all they ever do is paste AI output, you are training the exact employee you say you cannot promote.
  • Say the quiet part out loud in your team too. If AI is changing hiring, your juniors deserve to know what is actually valued now.

You do not need all nine sections. Pick one proof project and start it this week.

The real pattern

Every industrial shift eats the bottom of the ladder first, and every time, the survivors are the people who move one rung up faster than the machine. AI is not ending careers. It is ending the era when a career started with years of routine work nobody watched. The new first rung is judgement, proof and supervision, and it is harder, lonelier and more valuable than the old one. The companies that forget this will wake up in ten years with no seniors, because they stopped growing juniors today.

Final thoughts

The ladder lost its first rung, but it did not lose its top. Climb to the judgement work, ship proof nobody can fake, and learn to supervise the machine instead of competing with it. That is the job now, and it is still wide open.
