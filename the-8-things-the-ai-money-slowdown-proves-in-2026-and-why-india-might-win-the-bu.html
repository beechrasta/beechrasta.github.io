The 8 Things the AI Money Slowdown Proves in 2026, and Why India Might Win the Bust

The AI trade sucked the air out of every other market on earth. If it exhales, India is standing right there with its lungs open.

Marine Drive, Mumbai, at dusk. Indian markets rose while global tech sold off, and a new report says the money leaving the AI trade could head here. Photo by Sid Saxena on Unsplash.

Let's stop pretending the AI boom is a technology story. The technology works. The financing is the part that is starting to groan.

Last week, Wall Street wiped about $2.5 trillion off the Nasdaq 100 in a single day. This week, a report from SBI Funds says the money fleeing that trade could be heading to India.

This article is not a market prediction. It is about what happens when the biggest spending boom in history meets the bond market, and why the winner of the bust might not be an AI company at all.

1. The number that frames the whole bubble

  • A report from SBI Funds says artificial intelligence capital expenditure is projected to average around 3.6% of US GDP between 2025 and 2032.
  • Compare that with the two great spending booms people always cite. The dotcom cycle peaked at 1.1% of GDP in 2000. The 19th century railroad boom peaked at 2.2%.
  • In other words, the AI buildout is not a little bigger than the dotcom boom. It is more than three times bigger, measured against the size of the economy paying for it.
  • The report's warning is plain: if AI revenues come into doubt, sustaining capex at current financing costs could come under question, and the effects would spread to related sectors, US equities, US growth, and how money is allocated across asset classes.
  • Every boom looks affordable until someone asks who pays for the next round.

(Source: Telangana Today)

2. Last Thursday the market already voted

  • On Thursday, November 5, the Nasdaq 100 plunged 6.36%, erasing approximately $2.5 trillion in market value in one session, according to Nasdaq index data. It was the index's steepest one day drop since March 2020.
  • The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.2%. Nvidia lost about 9%. AMD and Micron each shed more than 17%. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.5% and closed in correction territory, nearly 12% below its record close on October 6.
  • The trigger, by most accounts, was the US midterm outcome, which raised the prospect of tighter scrutiny of data centre construction in Texas, Georgia, and Ohio. But the selling had a deeper cause: investors were already questioning whether the AI buildout can be financed and monetised on the promised schedule.
  • And here is the detail that matters for this story. While global tech stocks tumbled, Indian equities bucked the worldwide sell off with a sharp rally on November 6.
  • The market did not just sell AI. It started shopping for somewhere else to put the money.

(Source: The Hindu)

A trader watching the screens. The Nasdaq 100 lost $2.5 trillion in a single session on November 5. Image: The Investing Course.

3. The debt is the story now

  • The AI buildout stopped being funded out of cash flow a while ago. It is now funded out of debt, and the debt market has started to notice.
  • Hyperscaler debt issuance hit $121 billion in 2025, four times the historical average, and Bank of America expects another $100 billion in 2026, according to Financial Times reporting.
  • PIMCO estimates hyperscaler capex now absorbs 93% to 94% of operating cash flow, before any of that debt financing even enters the picture.
  • The stress is visible in individual names. CoreWeave, the Nvidia backed cloud company running 51 data centers, carries roughly $52 billion in debt and lease obligations, and its bonds fell below 80 cents on the dollar in mid October, pushing the yield toward 15%.
  • Oracle's Project Jupiter, a 1,400 acre AI data center campus in New Mexico, has about $18 billion in loans trading at 89 to 91 cents on the dollar after permit denials, environmental litigation, and construction delays pushed the timeline back at least seven months. Oracle has invoked force majeure, and its credit rating slipped to BBB-, one notch above junk.
  • Meta's Hyperion campus tells the same story at the top of the market. Bonds tied to the 5 gigawatt Louisiana site traded at 91 cents on the dollar, pushing their yield to about 7.55%. The $27 billion offering was the largest private bond deal ever, rated A+.
  • Bloomberg estimates there is more than $500 billion of AI data center debt outstanding, about $200 billion of it held by private credit funds. Morgan Stanley puts the off balance sheet financing and leasing of Nvidia, Broadcom, and the hyperscalers at about $3 trillion.
  • Booms do not end when the technology fails. They end when the financing does.

(Source: Startup Fortune)

4. What capital rotation actually means

  • Here is the sentence from the SBI Funds report that does all the work: the AI trade has sucked in capital from most other assets, and any weakening of that trade should mean capital moving to other areas.
  • Think about what that means mechanically. For two years, the AI trade has been the only trade. Pension funds, insurers, and asset managers who wanted growth had one address. If that address stops delivering, the money does not vanish. It relocates.
  • Bank of America estimates US AI related corporate bond issuance reached roughly $655 billion by mid October this year, after about $394 billion in 2025, with analysts expecting it to approach $1 trillion next year.
  • Goldman Sachs estimates hyperscalers need around $300 billion a year in AI revenue just to break even on their spending. That is the bar the revenue has to clear, every year, for the financing to make sense.
  • Morgan Stanley expects private credit to supply more than half of the $1.5 trillion the buildout needs through 2028. When the funding of the biggest industrial project in history depends on private credit funds, the question is not whether the technology works. It is whether the lenders stay calm.

(Source: The State of AI)

5. Why India, why now

  • The SBI Funds report does not argue India is a better AI story than the US. It argues India is a better capital story when the AI trade weakens, and the October numbers back that up.
  • Indian indices ended higher in October amid a sharp sell off across emerging markets. The Nifty gained about 1.9% and the Sensex about 2.7% in the month, making India one of the few major markets to advance.
  • India's merchandise exports rose about 23% in the third quarter of 2026, supported by the global trade recovery and India's improved tariff position relative to its peers.
  • Economic indicators in India have held up well despite elevated energy prices and their impact on inflation and fiscal conditions, the report noted.
  • Brent crude plunged to below $100 from $115 a barrel in October after the midterm outcome, which is the kind of move that quietly improves India's import bill and its inflation outlook at the same time.
  • The case is not that India is winning the AI race. It is that India is the market that held up while the AI trade wobbled, and money remembers that.

(Source: Lokmat Times)

6. The catch nobody puts in the headline

  • Read the SBI Funds report honestly and the whole argument is conditional. It says India could benefit, potentially benefit, if AI revenues come into doubt. Every key verb is a maybe.
  • A slow bleed in AI spending is very different from a crash. A slow bleed rotates capital gradually and rewards the steady markets. A crash panics everyone at once, and in a panic, emerging markets usually sell off first, not last.
  • India also has its own exposure to the AI story. Its IT services giants, its startup ecosystem, and its data centre buildout all depend on the same hyperscaler spending the report says might slow. A world where Microsoft and Amazon cut capex is not automatically a world where Indian tech hiring booms.
  • And the report is a fund house's market note, not a prophecy. It is a plausible reading of capital flows from an institution with an interest in those flows.
  • So the honest version of the story is narrower than the headline: if the AI trade keeps weakening in an orderly way, India is positioned to catch some of the money. That is a real argument. It is not a guarantee.

(Source: Telangana Today)

7. What this means if you work in Indian tech

  • If you are a developer, the money flow decides which infrastructure gets built, which tools get funded, and where the jobs land. A rotation toward India means more data centre work, more cloud migration work, and more global capability centre hiring, not necessarily more frontier model work.
  • If you are in IT services, your clients are the same hyperscalers whose capex is under question. Watch their earnings calls, not the headlines. The first sign of a real slowdown will be a delayed data centre, not a press release.
  • If you are a founder, a rotation is a funding window. Capital that leaves the AI trade has to go somewhere, and Indian startups that sell picks and shovels, cost optimisation, and applied AI are the ones that look cheap next to a $500 billion debt pile.
  • If you are an investor, the lesson of last Thursday is diversification, not timing. The Nasdaq 100 fell 6.4% in a day. The Sensex rose. Nobody predicted that split a month ago.
  • The skill is not betting on the bust. The skill is being employable, investable, and useful in whichever market the money lands in next.

(Source: AInvest)

8. Every boom ends with the money looking for a new address

  • The SBI Funds report reaches for history for a reason. The railroad boom ran at 2.2% of GDP. The dotcom boom ran at 1.1%. The AI boom is running at 3.6%. Every one of those booms ended the same way: the spending outran the revenue, the financing tightened, and the money moved.
  • After the railroads, the money moved into steel and oil. After dotcom, it moved into the companies that survived the crash and the infrastructure they left behind. The winners of the bust are rarely the builders of the boom.
  • Citigroup has actually raised its forecast for AI infrastructure spending by tech giants to more than $2.8 trillion through 2029, and Morgan Stanley puts the global data centre capex need at $2.9 trillion between 2025 and 2028 against a funding shortfall it estimates at $1.5 trillion. The bulls and the bears agree on one thing: the numbers are enormous either way.
  • The question is not whether AI is real. The question is whether $3.6% of GDP of spending, financed increasingly with debt, can be sustained long enough for the revenue to catch up. Goldman's $300 billion a year break even number is the test, and nobody knows the answer yet.
  • India is not the answer to that question. India is where the money goes while it waits for one.

How to start

  • If you are a developer in India: learn the infrastructure layer, not just the model layer. Cloud cost optimisation, data centre operations, and applied AI integration are the skills a rotation rewards first.
  • If you invest: stop checking the Nasdaq every morning and start watching hyperscaler bond yields and capex guidance. The bond market will call the turn months before the stock market admits it.
  • If you run a startup: this is the window to raise on applied AI revenue, not on model hype. Investors rotating out of the AI trade want cash flow, not demos.
  • If you just want to understand the news: remember the two numbers. 3.6% of GDP going in, $300 billion a year needed coming out. Everything else is commentary.

The real pattern

Every boom this size ends the same way, and it is never the technology that fails first. The railroads worked. The internet worked. AI works. What fails is the financing, because spending three times the dotcom boom's share of GDP has to be paid for by someone, and the bond market is the only lender big enough to try. When it starts charging 15% to the companies building the future, the future gets more expensive, the money looks for a cheaper address, and right now that address might be India.

Final thoughts

The AI boom is not over. But the easy money part of it might be.

And in every bust, somebody's market goes up while everyone else is watching the crash. This time, that market might be ours.
