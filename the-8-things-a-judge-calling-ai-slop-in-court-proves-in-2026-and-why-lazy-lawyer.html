The 8 Things a Judge Calling AI Slop in Court Proves in 2026, and Why Lazy Lawyering Just Got a Name

A federal judge looked at three lawyers' AI-written court filings and gave the whole profession a phrase it will never live down.

![]

A wooden judge's gavel on its sounding block. Judge Mark Pittman called the AI-written filings in front of him "blatant AI slop". Photo by Sasun Bughdaryan on Unsplash.

Let's stop pretending AI in the courtroom is a future problem. It is a right now problem, and this week a judge in Texas said the quiet part out loud.

Judge Mark T. Pittman of the US District Court for the Northern District of Texas called the filings in front of him "blatant AI slop" and "lazy lawyering", then denied the motion they were attached to. The lawyers had even disclosed that they used generative AI. It did not save them.

This article is not about one bad motion. It is about the moment the legal system stopped being polite about AI-generated garbage.

1. The phrase that ended the argument

  • Judge Pittman wrote that he was "disturbed by counsel's repeated filings which subtly bend the law by proffering superficially persuasive but fundamentally meritless arguments so clearly generated by artificial intelligence."
  • He called the work "blatant AI slop", a phrase no judge had ever put in a court order before.
  • The order came down on Wednesday, November 4, denying a motion to release a defendant from detention while he awaits sentencing.
  • Pittman also warned that any further waste of the court's and the government's limited resources could bring consequences, and noted his docket allows no hearing before the Thanksgiving holiday.

Judges have fined lawyers for AI hallucinations before. This is the first time one named the product for what it is, slop, and aimed it at the lawyers, not the tool.

"Blatant AI slop" is now in the federal record, and every lawyer in America just felt it.

(Source: Bloomberg Law)

2. The case behind the quote

  • The defendant is Khalwinder Singh Bhengura, a 70-year-old Indian national and lawful permanent resident of the United States.
  • A jury convicted him last month of making false claims of citizenship to register to vote and illegally casting a ballot in a federal election.
  • His defense team, attorneys Mario Herrera, Nicole Knox, and Vincent Rene Carrizales, filed motions asking for his release from detention pending sentencing.
  • The judge found the motions mostly generated by artificial intelligence, and legally flawed.

Notice what the case is actually about. A 70-year-old man faces sentencing for voting illegally, and the thing that may have hurt him most was not the charge. It was his own lawyers handing the judge a stack of AI-written arguments that bent the law just enough to be useless.

The client pays for the lawyer's shortcut. That is the part nobody in the AI hype talks about.

(Source: Business Insider via BizToc)

3. They disclosed the AI use, and it still did not save them

  • The defense team explicitly disclosed that they used a generative AI tool to prepare their motions.
  • Pittman acknowledged the disclosure, and then slammed the work anyway.
  • The problem was not secrecy. The problem was that the arguments were "superficially persuasive but fundamentally meritless."
  • The judge's point was blunt: telling the court you used AI does not make bad law good law.

This is the detail that matters most for everyone building or selling legal AI tools. Disclosure is not a shield. A disclosed hallucination is still a hallucination, and a disclosed meritless argument is still meritless.

The court does not care that you were honest about the shortcut. It cares that you took one.

(Source: ViralBrief)

4. This is not the first courtroom, it is a pattern now

  • In June 2026, Judge Sharion Aycock in Mississippi booted four lawyers, two from each side, from a contract dispute after finding AI hallucinations in both sides' filings.
  • Two of those lawyers were barred from appearing before that court for two years. The judge called the lawyers "a rubber stamp" for unverified AI output.
  • In 2025, Judge Nina Y. Wang fined two lawyers for Mike Lindell $3,000 each for a filing with nearly 30 defective citations, including cases that do not exist.
  • In Indiana, a magistrate judge recommended a $10,000 sanction for a lawyer whose briefs contained hallucinated citations, the third time in a year he had faced the same issue in that court.

Texas is not an outlier. It is the latest stop on a tour that has now hit Mississippi, Colorado, Indiana, and Wyoming, and the judges are getting less patient each time.

When four different courts in one year have to invent punishments for the same mistake, it is not a mistake anymore. It is a business model.

(Source: Mississippi Free Press)

5. The Mississippi case where both sides got booted

  • The case was a simple contract dispute in Aberdeen, Mississippi, between attorney Tom Withers III and the city.
  • In December 2025, Judge Aycock warned both sides she had "serious concerns" that their memos contained fictitious legal authorities.
  • At a January hearing, both sides admitted it. One lawyer admitted using AI to draft the filing. Another admitted signing memos without checking the sources.
  • On June 8, 2026, Aycock issued sanctions, cleared both legal teams entirely, and wrote that the case was "a prime example of the risk associated with serving as a rubber-stamp" in "an era of rampant unverified AI usage within the legal field."

Think about that. Two opposing teams, both using AI, both filing fake law, arguing against each other in front of a judge who had to fire all of them. The AI did not pick a side. It just made everyone look incompetent at the same time.

The funniest and the saddest part is the same: the AI argued with itself, and both sides lost.

(Source: Mississippi Free Press)

6. The fines are small, the record is forever

  • The Lindell lawyers paid $3,000 each. The Wyoming lawyer paid $3,000 and got kicked off his case.
  • The Indiana recommendation was $10,000. The earlier sanctions in that same court were $6,000 and $7,500.
  • These are parking-ticket numbers for lawyers who bill hundreds of dollars an hour.
  • But the orders are public, searchable, and permanent. "Blatant AI slop" will follow those three Texas lawyers into every Google search a future client runs.

Courts are not trying to bankrupt anyone. They are trying to embarrass the behavior out of existence, and embarrassment scales better than fines. A $3,000 fine is a bad afternoon. A federal order calling your work slop is a career footnote.

The punishment is not the money. The punishment is the quote.

(Source: FindLaw)

7. What this means for the developers building legal AI

  • Every legal AI startup promises to draft motions, summarize cases, and find citations in seconds.
  • The courts are now writing the product requirements in sanctions orders: verify every citation, or your user gets fined.
  • A tool that generates plausible-sounding law without a verification step is not a legal tool. It is a liability machine with a subscription fee.
  • The winning legal AI products will be the ones that show their work, cite real cases with real links, and refuse to invent anything when the database comes up empty.

If you are building AI for lawyers, Judge Pittman just handed you your roadmap. Build the verification layer first, the generation layer second. And if you are a lawyer using these tools, the rule is simple: you signed it, you own it, and "the AI wrote it" is not a defense in any courtroom in America.

The market for legal AI is not going away. The market for legal AI without guardrails just got its first obituary.

(Source: Bloomberg Law)

8. The real skill is not generating, it is verifying

  • AI can draft a motion in seconds. It cannot tell you whether the cases it cited exist.
  • Every sanction in this story has the same root cause: a human signed a document they did not read.
  • Judge Dinsmore in Indiana said it plainly, the issue "is not the use of AI to assist in legal work; it is the failure to review the validity and content of the cases cited in briefs filed with the Court."
  • The lawyers who survive this era will be the ones who treat AI output like a first draft from a careless intern: useful, fast, and never to be trusted without checking.

This is the lesson that extends far beyond law. Doctors, engineers, students, journalists, everyone who uses AI to produce work they sign their name to, the signature is the job now. Generating is free. Verifying is the profession.

AI did not make lawyers lazy. It made laziness visible, quotable, and sanctionable.

(Source: Reason, Volokh Conspiracy)

How to start

  • If you are a lawyer: never file anything you have not read line by line, including every citation. Check each case exists, and that it says what your motion claims it says.
  • If you are a law student: learn to use AI as a research assistant, not a ghostwriter. The lawyers getting sanctioned are your cautionary tale, and your professors are already watching for it.
  • If you build legal AI tools: ship the citation verifier before you ship the generator. A tool that refuses to invent a case is worth more than a tool that invents one beautifully.
  • If you use AI for any professional work: adopt the courtroom rule. Your name on it means you verified it, and "the AI wrote it" is not a defense anywhere that matters.

The real pattern

The courts are not fighting AI. They are fighting the oldest professional sin in the book, signing work you did not do, and AI just made that sin faster, cheaper, and easier to catch. Every profession is about to get its own Judge Pittman moment, the day someone in authority stops debating the technology and starts naming the behavior. The name they gave it this week was slop, and it is going to stick to a lot more than three lawyers in Texas.

Final thoughts

A judge calling your work "blatant AI slop" is not a technology story. It is a standards story. The tools will keep getting better, the drafts will keep getting smoother, and the temptation to skip the checking will keep getting stronger. The only thing standing between a professional and a sanctions order is the ten minutes it takes to verify what the machine just wrote. Spend the ten minutes.
